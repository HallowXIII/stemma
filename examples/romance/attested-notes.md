## Disagreements between sources
1. **OF ai → ɛ.** Nyrop (I §200) says ai was already monophthongal [ɛ] in the 12th century, with the Roland fluctuating, and that verb-final *ai* (chanterai, ai) went to [e]. Wikipedia dates ai > ɛ to c.1250–1300. This affects *aim/aimes/aiment*, *ai*, *chanterai* and *fraile*. I kept /aj/ and flagged it. Before a nasal (*aim*), the diphthong probably lasted longer, merging with ẽi.
2. **OF ei → oi.** Bourciez (§54) says it was complete in the Centre and East "vers le milieu du XIIe siècle". Wikipedia says c.1100. Wiktionary labels *aveir* archaic and *avoir* classical. Francien c.1150 sits right on the line, so I chose *avoir* /avojr/ and gave *aveir* /avejr/ as the alternative. *plein* is unaffected because a nasal blocks the change.
3. **Quality of the OF infinitive vowel -er (< free stressed a).** Wiktionary gives /eː/ and Wikipedia gives /æ/. Pope-style accounts treat it as a separate assonance class from ę and ẹ. I wrote /e/.
4. **OF 3sg -et.** Bourciez says the final dental was "tombé dès le début du XIIe siècle". Nyrop (II §115.3) says it was kept in *Alexis* (c.1050) and already disappearing in the Roland, although MS O writes it everywhere. Wikipedia dates the loss of /θ ð/ to c.1100 or the 12th century. All three agree it was gone in Francien by 1150, so I wrote /tʃantə/ and gave *chantet* as a spelling only. The same applies to the intervocalic dental fricative [ð]: Wikipedia's Old French article says it had disappeared by about 1100.
5. **OF avoir 3sg.** Wikipedia's *Old French* table (as extracted) gives a 3sg *ai* and a 2sg *ais*, which looks like an error. Wiktionary and the standard paradigm give *a, at*, and I used those.
6. **OF homo, nom.sg.** Nyrop's paradigm writes the nom.sg as *on*. Wiktionary's headword is *hom*, with variants *om, on, hum, hon, huem*. The diphthongised *huem* (Bourciez) is non-Francien. Both the vowel (ɔ vs o) and the final nasal (m vs n) are uncertain.
7. **OSp 'man'.** Wiktionary and Pidal both give *omne* as the normal OSp form. Pidal's Cid grammar says the Cid (and the Auto de los Magos) use *omne*, written *ome* with a tilde, and that *ombre* is exceptional. *omre* and *ombre* belong later in the 13th–14th centuries. I found no source that dates *omre* specifically.
8. **fragile.** fr.wiktionary dates it to 1370. en.wiktionary calls OF *fraile* a "doublet of OF *fragile*", which implies an earlier OF *fragile*, but it gives no date. TLFi would settle this but I couldn't reach it.

## Judgement calls for c.1150 OF / c.1200 OSp
- **OF rr in *terre*.** Bourciez (§181) says double rr was still long and trilled in the 16th century, so I wrote a geminate /rr/. Drop it if your model has no length.
- **OF *chievre*.** It is not settled whether the ie was falling [ie̯] or rising [je] by 1150, nor whether the e was close or open. I wrote /tʃje/. Bourciez says ie → e after ch/g in the 14th–15th centuries.
- **OF -ons.** I wrote /ons/. Some notations use /uns/ or /ũns/. Nyrop (§55) derives it from *-umus* spreading from *sumus* and estons, and notes it was already present in St Léger (10th c.).
- **Nasalisation.** Left unmarked throughout, as allophonic.
- **Affricates.** tʃ, dʒ and ts (written *z* in *chantez*, *amez*) were all still affricates c.1150. Deaffrication came c.1250–1300 (Wikipedia).
- **OSp /b/ vs /β/.** *cabra* (< -p-) has /b/ and *aver* (< -b-) has /β/. This follows the traditional (Penny/Alarcos) account and the b vs u/v spelling contrast. I did not check it against a source in this session.
- **OSp future.** Synthetic *cantaré* and the split *cantar lo he* co-exist c.1200. The TSV gives the synthetic form.
- **OSp he/ha.** MSS often write *e*/*a* without h. Pidal derives them from VL *haio, *hat. Archaic *heo, hey* are not 1200 Castilian.
- **French 1sg -e.** Nyrop (II §115.1) says it spread from the 12th century, but e-less 1sg forms (*je vous aim, je conseil*) still appear in 15th-century poetry, and *pri* survives in Hardy (c.1600). So chant → chante was not a single event: it began in the 12th century and finished in the 16th.
- **aim-/am- levelling.** Nyrop says *amer, amons, amez* were replaced by *aimer, aimons, aimez* by analogy with *aime*, but gives no date. Middle French paradigms (Wiktionary *aymer*) already have *aymons/aymez*, so the change was essentially done by the 14th–15th centuries. I found no exact date.
- **Inherited Spanish reflex of fragilis.** I found none. Spanish *fraile* 'friar' comes from *frater* via Occitan.
